PDF Too Big to Email? How to Compress It Without Visible Loss

A 'file too large' bounce usually traces back to images inside the PDF. Set realistic expectations, remove what you do not need, then compress.

Email services typically cap attachments at 20 to 25 MB, and a scanned or image-heavy PDF passes that easily. Most PDFs can be reduced a lot with no loss you would notice.

Where the size comes from

Images dominate: high-resolution scans, photos and embedded pictures. Text takes almost no space, so compression mostly works by bringing images down to a sensible resolution.

Three steps

  1. Upload the PDF: Open the Compress tool and choose your file.
  2. Compress: The tool optimizes images and redundant data to reduce the size.
  3. Download the result: The smaller file fits an email or upload comfortably.

The biggest gains come from scans. Text-only PDFs are already small, so savings there are limited.

Try deleting before compressing

In scanned files, blank reverse sides, pages scanned twice and pointless cover sheets can take several megabytes. Removing them with the delete-pages tool and then compressing beats compression alone, and costs no quality.

What to expect by file type

  • Scanned documents: the biggest gains. High-resolution scans usually shrink a lot.
  • Image-heavy decks and catalogues: good gains, but watch the setting if image quality matters.
  • Text-only documents: already small, so you will not turn a 400 KB contract into 100 KB.
  • Files compressed before: a second pass gains little and costs quality.

If compression is not enough

Split the document into sensible parts and send two emails; the recipient gets the parts in order. For statements or reports, sending only the relevant page range reduces size and avoids sharing information nobody asked for.

Checking quality

Compression shows in print and at zoom, not at normal screen size. After downloading, open the page with the smallest type and zoom to 200%. If the letters are still legible, the file is usable. Do not skip this for documents going to an institution, because an illegible scan is among the most common reasons for rejection.

Keep files small from the start

  • Scan at 300 DPI rather than 600; more is unnecessary for text documents.
  • Do not scan black-and-white paperwork in colour; greyscale is usually enough and far smaller.
  • Run phone photos through the Document Scanner instead of converting them to PDF directly.
  • Do not compress and re-save the same document repeatedly, since each round costs a little more quality.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my file still large after compressing?

A document with many high-resolution scans can stay large even after compression. Deleting unnecessary pages or splitting the document works better in that case.

Does compression damage the text?

No. The savings come from images and the text layer is untouched. Text stays selectable and searchable.

Does compressing twice shrink it more?

Usually not. A second pass yields a small gain while degrading image quality another step. Reaching the target in one pass is better.

Can I compress a signed PDF?

On documents with a digital signature, compression can invalidate the signature because the file content changes. Confirm whether the signature is required before compressing.

How do I reduce PDF size?

Upload the PDF to the Compress tool. It optimizes images to reduce size and you download the smaller file.

Does compression ruin quality?

The goal is the smallest size at a quality you will not notice. Scans shrink a lot with little visible loss.

Why is my PDF so large?

Usually high-resolution scans and photos. Compression brings those images down to a reasonable resolution.
