Where to look first in a contract

Reading a foreign-language contract end to end is usually unnecessary; the real risk concentrates in a handful of clauses. Once you have the translation, look for these headings first — most commercial contracts use similar names.

  • Parties and authority: who signs, and does the document show their authority?
  • Term and renewal: does it renew automatically, and how much notice prevents that?
  • Termination: in what circumstances and with how much notice can it end?
  • Payment: amount, currency, due dates and late interest.
  • Liability cap and indemnity: is there an upper limit on damages?
  • Governing law and jurisdiction: where and under which law will disputes be resolved?
  • Confidentiality and non-compete: duration and scope.

Why the governing law clause matters

The governing law and jurisdiction clause sits near the end and is often one of the shortest, yet in a dispute it can matter more than everything above it. Pursuing a claim in another country's courts can cost more than the value of the work the contract covers. Do not skip it while reading the translation, and clarify anything unclear by asking directly.

What to watch for in the translation

In legal text some terms differ from their everyday meaning, and a literal translation can mislead. In English contracts «shall» expresses obligation while «may» expresses discretion; terms like «indemnify», «warranty» and «best efforts» carry specific meanings tied to a legal system. A translation tells you what the text says; assessing its legal effect is a separate job.

Finding the difference between two versions

During negotiation the other side often sends an updated version saying they made minor edits. Rather than rereading the whole document, comparing the two versions is both faster and safer: added, removed and changed clauses show up one by one. This is how quiet changes to amounts, dates and liability clauses get caught.

When you need professional help

AI translation and summaries exist to help you understand a document and ask the right questions. They do not replace working with a lawyer on contracts you will sign, that involve significant amounts, or that bind you for a long time. Official procedures require a sworn translation; institutions do not accept machine translation.
