Contract Review Checklist: 12 Clauses to Read Before You Sign

Contracts rarely go wrong in the long, complicated clauses. They go wrong in a deadline buried in an annex or a termination right that only one side has. Twelve checks, grouped by what they protect.

If you have ever lost a dispute over a contract you had technically read, the cause was probably small: a different delivery date in an annex, a penalty with no ceiling, a right to terminate that belonged to the other party alone. This checklist groups twelve checks by what they protect, so you can work through a contract in a fixed order instead of rereading it hoping to notice something.

Group 1: Who is bound, and to what

  1. Parties and authority. Company names, tax numbers, and whether the person signing can actually bind the company.
  2. Subject and scope. What is delivered, in what quantity and to what standard. Phrases such as 'reasonable time' or 'as deemed necessary' are where arguments begin.
  3. Price and payment. VAT included or excluded, the payment term and the date it starts from. If invoicing waits for an approval, check whether that approval has its own deadline.
  4. Deadlines and notices. Delivery, objection, notice and non-renewal periods, and for each one who must notify whom and by what means (notary, email, registered post).

Group 2: What it costs when things go wrong

  1. Late-delivery penalty. The rate, whether there is a cap, and the amount it is applied to. If damages can be claimed on top of the penalty, treat the penalty as uncapped.
  2. Termination. Can both sides terminate? With how much notice? Is work already performed paid for?
  3. Liability. How are indirect loss, lost profit and gross negligence handled, and is the cap offered to one party only?
  4. Intellectual property and confidentiality. Do your designs, tooling or methods pass to the other side, and are the confidentiality exceptions mutual?

Group 3: The structure around the text

  1. Annexes. Do you hold every annex the contract mentions? If a clause says the annex prevails over the body, read the annexes first.
  2. Term and renewal. Does the contract renew by itself, and by when must you give notice to stop that?
  3. Disputes. Which court or arbitral body, and which governing law? A distant forum makes enforcement more expensive.
  4. What is absent. Force majeure, transfer of risk, warranty and defect periods, assignment, data protection. Missing standard clauses are findings too.

Convert every rate into a number before you decide. '1% per day' reads as harmless; on a 2,400,000 TL job it is 24,000 TL every day.

Working order

Do groups 2 and 3 first. Penalty, termination, payment and liability carry the biggest money, and annex conflicts decide which of your figures even apply. For every clause you flag, note the problem and a balanced replacement the other side could realistically accept; a marked-up contract with fixes gets further than one with objections alone.

This is a screening aid, not legal advice. On a high-value or multi-year agreement, take a lawyer's view before signing, and bring your flagged list so the conversation starts where your own review stopped.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which clause causes the most disputes?

Penalty and termination clauses, payment terms, and annexes that were never shared. Start there, since the financial effect is largest.

Is a standard-form contract negotiable?

At least the few riskiest clauses usually are. Asking for balance, such as mutual rights and a reasonable cap, is less likely to be refused outright than demanding a rewrite.
